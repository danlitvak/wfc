01 / THE IDEA

Every cell starts knowing nothing — and every possibility at once.

Wave Function Collapse borrows its name from quantum mechanics as a metaphor. At the start, each cell on the grid is in superposition: it could become any tile in the set. The algorithm repeatedly picks the most-constrained cell, observes it — forcing it into a single tile — and lets that decision ripple outward.

Underneath the physics costume it is really a humble constraint solver. Tiles carry rules about who may sit beside them; WFC just keeps choosing and pruning until every cell agrees.

Superposition
A cell holds all 16 tiles until something rules them out.
Observation
Collapsing a cell picks one tile, weighted by frequency.
Propagation
That choice removes options from the neighbours.
02 / ENTROPY & OBSERVATION

Always collapse the cell with the least to lose.

A cell's entropy is just how many tiles it could still become. WFC always observes the cell with the lowest entropy — the one closest to being decided — because committing there is least likely to cause a conflict. Ties are broken with a pinch of randomness, which is why every run looks different.

When a cell is observed, the winning tile is drawn from its survivors using their weights. Flip on Show entropy heat in the panel to watch the field cool from chaotic to certain in real time.

03 / PROPAGATION

One decision pushes a wave of consequences across the board.

The moment a cell collapses, its neighbours must drop any tile that no longer fits. If a neighbour loses options, its neighbours are re-checked too. The change propagates outward like a ripple — sometimes a single cell, sometimes a cascade that crosses the whole grid. The bright flashes you see are exactly this wave.

→

Observe

Pick the lowest-entropy cell and collapse it to one tile.

→

Propagate

Remove now-impossible tiles from neighbours, repeat until stable.

→

Repeat

Loop until every cell is decided — or a contradiction appears.

04 / THE TILES

Adjacency is the whole game — tiles connect only where edges agree.

This playground uses the classic pipes set: 16 tiles built from four edges that are each either open or closed. Two tiles may be neighbours only when their touching edges match — a line out the right side must meet a line coming in from the left. That single rule, applied everywhere, is enough to weave the whole network.

05 / CONTRADICTIONS

Sometimes a cell runs out of options. Then you start again.

Because choices are made locally, WFC can paint itself into a corner: a cell whose every tile has been eliminated. This is a contradiction. The simplest cure — and what this demo does — is to restart with a fresh seed. Smarter implementations backtrack, rewinding only the last few moves. The permissive pipes set rarely fails, but bump the resolution up and you may catch one in the act.
